## 4. Product Leadership Themes

Ian’s strongest product themes include legacy modernization, AI adoption, customer trust, hidden workflows, and operational intelligence.

### Migration as trust transfer

Ian has developed a strong framing around migrations.

The core idea:

> A customer migration is not just a data transfer. It is a trust transfer.

This is especially important when moving customers from legacy products to next-generation or AI-first products.

The risk is not only whether the data moves.

The deeper risk is whether the customer still believes:

- The new system will work.
- The company understands their business.
- Support will be responsive.
- Payroll and workforce operations will remain stable.
- Their prior investment is being respected.

This framing is central to Ian’s career strategy because it lets him talk about migration work in strategic, executive-relevant terms.

### Legacy systems and trapped ROI

Ian has written and thought about AI ROI being trapped inside legacy systems.

The core idea:

> AI’s ROI often remains trapped when companies build AI alongside the old way instead of replacing the old way.

This connects to Prakash Kota’s insight that AI adoption required “killing the old way.”

Ian’s view is that many companies are not failing because they lack AI tooling.

They are failing because they keep the legacy process alive next to the AI process.

That creates:

- Duplicate work.
- Trust gaps.
- Confusing ownership.
- Measurement noise.
- Slow adoption.
- Hidden friction.

Ian often uses analogies like Apple removing the headphone jack to explain the hard part of product adoption.

Sometimes the value is only released when the old path is intentionally removed.

### Building the wrong thing faster

Ian is interested in a sharper critique of AI adoption:

> AI does not automatically make organizations smarter. It can help them build the wrong thing faster.

This theme shows up in his product and culture writing.

The point is not anti-AI.

The point is that AI magnifies the quality of the system around it.

If the workflow is broken, AI can accelerate the brokenness.

If the signal is poor, AI can amplify poor decisions.

If the problem is badly framed, AI can make bad execution look efficient.

### TTRE and support workflow intelligence

Ian has a real support workflow example that shaped one of his strongest AI use-case ideas.

A customer experienced a severe issue after an environment upgrade.

The issue prevented the customer from completing necessary payroll tasks.

The case was logged with support at 8:30 AM.

Ian’s team did not become aware of the emergency until 2:30 PM.

Engineering became involved after 3:00 PM.

The issue was resolved after 7:00 PM.

Ian identified this as a workflow visibility and escalation problem.

He wants to map the actual flow of the incident and identify where AI could reduce the time between signal and action.

The key metric he developed is **TTRE**, or time to resolution escalation / time to relevant engagement depending on framing.

The central idea:

> Leadership already cares about margins and churn. TTRE gives them a sharper way to see how hidden workflow delays create customer risk.

Potential AI-assisted improvements include:

- Detecting payroll-critical language in support cases.
- Flagging emergency operational impact.
- Routing incidents to product and engineering earlier.
- Summarizing customer impact.
- Triggering escalation workflows.
- Reducing awareness-to-resolution time.
- Creating executive visibility into support failure points.

Ian wants to turn this into a business case, one-pager, dashboard concept, and leadership briefing.

## 12. Writing Structure

Ian’s strongest writing often follows a three-act structure.

### Act 1: Emotional hook

Start with a lived tension, real moment, or sharp observation.

Examples of the kind of opening Ian likes:

- “Stop me if you’ve seen this one before.”
- “The numbers are impressive. But the gap is the story.”
- “The problem was not the dashboard. The problem was the operating system underneath it.”

The hook should feel human before it becomes analytical.

### Act 2: Analytical zoom-out

Move from the moment to the system.

This is where Ian connects:

- A workplace moment to a broader pattern.
- A product problem to a culture problem.
- An AI adoption issue to a trust issue.
- A migration problem to a business risk.

### Act 3: Humanizing bridge

End by bringing the idea back to people.

Ian does not like conclusions that only sound clever.

He wants the reader to feel why the idea matters.

The ending often reconnects to:

- Trust.
- Belonging.
- Confidence.
- Leadership.
- Visibility.
- Human stakes.

## 38. How to Write Like Ian

Write as if speaking to a smart colleague in the hallway after a meeting where everyone felt the problem but nobody named it.

Start with the tension.

Use a real moment or concrete example.

Zoom out to the system.

Connect it to product, culture, trust, or business value.

Bring it back to the human stakes.

Avoid sounding like a keynote speaker.

Avoid sounding like a consultant deck.

Avoid generic AI language.

Use precise, grounded, conversational language.

Make the reader feel like the thing they sensed is finally visible.

### Example voice direction

Not this:

> Organizations must leverage cross-functional synergies to accelerate AI transformation and unlock scalable value.

More like this:

> The tool was never the hard part. The hard part was getting the old workflow out of the way long enough for people to trust the new one.
